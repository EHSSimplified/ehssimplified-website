# The Hidden Cost of Skipping Breaks (And What It's Doing to Your Premium)

Fatigue costs U.S. employers an estimated $136 billion a year in lost productivity — and that number doesn't even count the claims. If your crew is running long hours with no real downtime, you're not saving time. You're financing your next workers' comp increase.

## The Numbers Behind "Just Push Through"

Fatigue isn't a soft issue. It shows up directly in injury data:

- Accident and injury rates run 18% higher on evening shifts and 30% higher on night shifts compared to a standard day shift.
- Workers pulling 12-hour days face a 37% higher injury risk than those on standard shifts.
- Every extra long shift in a month raises the odds of a crash on the way home by over 16%.

None of that requires a night crew or a factory floor to matter. A framing crew racing to finish before a storm, a plow driver on hour 14 of a snow event, a shop running back-to-back overtime to hit a delivery date — all of it adds up the same way.

## How This Lands on Your Insurance Bill

Every claim you file feeds your Experience Modification Rate, or EMR — the number insurers use to compare your claims history to other businesses in your trade. An EMR of 1.0 is average. Go above it, and every dollar of payroll gets more expensive to insure.

Here's the part most owners miss: EMR math rewards fewer claims more than it punishes one bad one. A handful of smaller, fatigue-driven strains and sprains can hurt your rate worse than a single serious incident — and that number follows you for three to four years, showing up every renewal, every bid, every certificate of insurance you hand a GC.

## Regulators Are Heading This Direction Too

OSHA's proposed heat illness rule — still not finalized, but actively moving — would require mandatory paid 15-minute rest breaks every two hours once the heat index hits 90°F, plus water and shade access starting at 80°F. It's not law yet. But it tells you where enforcement expectations are going, and NH crews already push through some brutal stretches during the short summer building season when every job is racing the same construction calendar.

Building the habit now — before it's mandatory — means you're not scrambling to write a policy the week a rule takes effect.

## A Break Policy That Actually Holds Up

You don't need a 20-page fatigue management program. You need something your foremen will actually follow:

- Set a real break cadence for extended shifts — 15 minutes every two hours is a reasonable baseline, not just for heat.
- Flag any day scheduled past 10-12 hours and build in a recovery break before the highest-risk task of the day, not after.
- Rotate crews off the most fatigue-heavy tasks (ladders, lifting, machinery) during the back half of a long shift.
- Track overtime by person, not just by job — a crew member stacking OT across multiple sites is a different risk than one long push on a single project.
- Make hydration and shade non-negotiable on hot days, regardless of what the final OSHA rule ends up requiring.

None of this slows a job down as much as owners assume. A 15-minute break costs you 15 minutes. A fatigue-driven injury costs you the claim, the EMR hit, the light-duty scramble, and the next three years of higher premiums.
