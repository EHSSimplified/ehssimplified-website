# Why We Really Break the Rules (And What It's Costing All of Us)

Here's a number that matters whether you sign the paychecks or run the buffer after everyone else goes home: a business with a 1.25 EMR — the rating insurers use to price workers' comp — pays $10,000 more a year than one sitting at 1.0, on a $40,000 base premium alone. Push that rating to 1.55, and the gap widens to $22,000. None of that is bad luck. It's built one shortcut at a time, by people who were never told why the rule existed in the first place.

You can post the signage, run the safety meetings, and hand out the PPE. But if nobody explains *why* a rule exists, people aren't following a safety program — they're just trying not to get caught. That gap costs everyone in the building something, whether or not their name is on the insurance policy.

## Why We Work the Way We Do

Nobody signs up for this kind of work expecting an easy schedule. Whether you're running the business, leading a crew, working the shop floor, or locking up after the last truck pulls out, you show up early, put in long hours, and make real sacrifices to keep things moving — because that's what getting it done takes, and because someone's counting on you to deliver.

That mindset is what makes a good team good. But it also means speed quietly becomes the measure of value — the person who finishes early, the shift that never falls behind, the supervisor who doesn't want to be the one explaining a missed deadline. When "get it done" is the only value anyone talks about, safety becomes the thing you do *if there's time* — not part of how the work gets done. People pick up on that instantly, whether you say it out loud or not.

## Why We "Follow" the Rules (Or Don't)

Here's an uncomfortable truth: most safety rules were written for roughly 10% of the workforce — the people who got hurt, or nearly did, on that specific task. The other 90% read the rule as generic policy that doesn't apply to them, because nobody ever told them the actual story behind it.

That's the real reason rules get ignored — by anyone, at any level. It's rarely defiance. It's that "corporate says so" doesn't hold up against a deadline, while "Mike broke his wrist doing exactly this in 2023, and it cost us a $9,000 claim" does. When people understand the why, compliance stops being something you have to enforce and starts being something they choose.

## Why We Take the Shortcut Anyway

Shortcuts don't usually start as a decision — they start as a habit. A step gets skipped because nothing happened last time. It works again. And again. Every successful shortcut trains the brain to treat the risk as smaller than it actually is.

That's part of why roughly 40% of days-away-from-work injuries happen to employees with five-plus years on the job, even though experienced workers get hurt less often overall. The rule didn't change. The conditions did — a slicker ladder rung, a jammed machine guard, a rushed afternoon — and the shortcut that "always worked" finally didn't. It doesn't matter what your job title is. The habit forms the same way for everyone.

## What Ignoring "Why" Costs Everyone at Renewal

This is where the psychology turns into a number on a page — and it's worth knowing even if you're not the one who signs the policy. Insurers are already pulling the "loss runs" — the claims history report that feeds the EMR — to price next year's renewal, and that data lags months behind. Which means the shortcuts happening on the floor or the job site this fall, as the New England season turns, are shaping the quote that lands this winter.

It shows up past the renewal too. General contractors increasingly set EMR cutoffs — often 1.2, sometimes 1.0 on government work — before a subcontractor can even bid. Manufacturers and shops run into the same wall from larger customers who now build safety and insurance checks into vendor qualification. A shaky safety culture doesn't just raise the premium — it can quietly take the whole team out of the running for the next contract, before anyone reviews the price. That's not only an owner's problem. That's hours, raises, and job security for everyone on the payroll.

## Building a Culture Around "Why" — For Everyone in the Building

You don't need a bigger rulebook. You need every rule to carry its reason with it, for every person who has to follow it:

- Tell the real story behind each rule in your safety meetings — the actual incident or claim that created it, not "because policy says so."
- Ask everyone, not just crew leads, where they're already cutting corners — before an incident forces the conversation. Treat honest answers as useful data, not confessions.
- Put a real number next to safety talks — what a claim costs, what it does to the EMR — instead of a general "be careful out there."
- Recognize the person who flags a near miss or a risky shortcut before it becomes a claim, whatever their role. That's the behavior you actually want more of.
- Retire any rule that's lost its "why." Dead-weight rules with no real story behind them make people tune out the ones that matter.
